# Logical chain

The argument of the paper "Beyond RLHF: A Unified Theoretical Framework of Alignment" (Yun et al.), from the problem it starts with to what it concludes. A reader with only this file should be able to follow the paper.

Each step links the key points it rests on. A paragraph marked "Supplementary" is background a reader needs at that step; it is not a claim of the paper unless its source says so.

## Why the paper questions the standard method

1. Aligning a language model (LM) means improving its responses using human preferences between pairs of responses. The standard method, RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback), first trains a reward model on those preferences, then trains the LM to maximize the reward while a penalty of weight β keeps it close to a reference LM. Other methods and most theory take this objective as the goal. ([Introduction, key point 1](yun2025/sections/introduction.md#key-point-1))

2. But the paper argues that this objective has never been justified. Existing guarantees only show that training reaches the objective's own solution; the definition of alignment says nothing about rewards; and the penalty poses a dilemma: with more data it should shrink to zero, but then the solution collapses to a single response per prompt, while keeping it holds learning back. These are arguments, not results. ([Introduction, key point 2](yun2025/sections/introduction.md#key-point-2))

3. And the guarantees that exist cannot be compared, because each method is analyzed toward a different target. So the paper asks: under what assumption about how preferences arise can training objectives be justified, or new ones derived? (Introduction, key points [3](yun2025/sections/introduction.md#key-point-3) and [4](yun2025/sections/introduction.md#key-point-4))

## The one assumption

4. The paper's answer starts from a single assumption. There is a target LM, and the chance that response a is preferred to response b depends only on how probable the target LM makes each of them: a's probability, raised to a power γ, divided by the same quantity summed over a and b. Alignment then becomes learning the target LM's distribution from preference pairs. ([Introduction, key point 5](yun2025/sections/introduction.md#key-point-5); Preliminaries, key points [1](yun2025/sections/preliminaries.md#key-point-1) and [2](yun2025/sections/preliminaries.md#key-point-2); [setup](yun2025/theorems/setup.md))

   Supplementary (from the paper, `[§1, Our contributions ¶1]`): this is a Bradley-Terry model, a standard way to turn a score for each item into a probability that one beats another. In RLHF the score is a separate reward; here it is the target LM's own probability.

5. The paper argues that this assumption is not too restrictive: the Bradley-Terry form is conventional, and tying the score to the target LM is what makes guarantees possible. It reports no test of whether human preferences follow it. ([Preliminaries, key point 3](yun2025/sections/preliminaries.md#key-point-3))

6. To prove anything it adds three conditions, two of which it calls standard: the target lies in a finite class of candidate LMs, a boundedness condition, and coverage. Coverage is needed because the responses people compared were generated by some other LM, not by the target. (Preliminaries, key points [4](yun2025/sections/preliminaries.md#key-point-4) and [5](yun2025/sections/preliminaries.md#key-point-5))

   Supplementary (from the paper, Definition 3, p.4): the coverage coefficient measures how well the responses in the data represent what the target LM would generate. The worse the match, the larger the coefficient, and the larger every error bound below.

## Three objectives that follow from it

Conditions for every guarantee below ([setup](yun2025/theorems/setup.md)): the class of candidate LMs is finite and contains the target; the learned LM is the exact minimizer of its objective; the penalty toward the reference LM is set to zero (β = 0); and the bounds hide constants that grow exponentially in the boundedness constant R.

Supplementary (not from the inputs): every guarantee bounds the forward KL divergence from the target to the learned LM, a standard measure of how far one distribution is from another. It is zero only when the two agree.

7. The first objective is the most direct: choose the LM under which the observed preference labels are most likely. The paper calls it PMLE (preference maximum likelihood). It resembles the existing method DPO, and in practice adds a penalty toward the reference LM that DPO lacks. Its error is at most of order C_Π·ln|Π| / (γ²·n): it shrinks as 1/n in the number n of preference pairs, and grows with the coverage coefficient C_Π and the size |Π| of the candidate class. (§3, key points [1](yun2025/sections/preference-mle.md#key-point-1) to [4](yun2025/sections/preference-mle.md#key-point-4); [Theorem 4](yun2025/theorems/theorem-4.md))

8. The second objective brings in a reward model without reinforcement learning. Under the assumption, a reward model is an LM in disguise, one that can score responses but not generate them. So the paper trains a reward model, treats it as a simulator of preferences, and fits the LM to the preference probabilities it gives. This preference distillation resembles the existing method REBEL, with a loss derived from the assumption in place of REBEL's squared loss. (§4, key points [1](yun2025/sections/preference-distillation.md#key-point-1) to [4](yun2025/sections/preference-distillation.md#key-point-4))

9. Its guarantee needs one more assumption, that the LMs the reward models stand for lie inside the candidate class, and it is then no better than PMLE's. It becomes better only if the LM is trained on responses from a stronger base LM, a condition the paper states and does not examine. (§4, key points [5](yun2025/sections/preference-distillation.md#key-point-5) to [7](yun2025/sections/preference-distillation.md#key-point-7); [Theorem 6](yun2025/theorems/theorem-6.md); [Theorem 14](yun2025/theorems/theorem-14.md))

10. The third objective measures closeness in the other direction, the reverse KL divergence. That needs the target LM's probabilities, which are unknown, so the reward model's estimate is substituted. The result is the RLHF objective plus one extra term: γ times the entropy of the LM. The paper calls it RKL. (§5, key points [1](yun2025/sections/reverse-kl.md#key-point-1) and [2](yun2025/sections/reverse-kl.md#key-point-2))

    Supplementary (from the paper, `[§5 ¶1]`): forward KL is "mode-covering" and reverse KL "mode-seeking". Entropy measures how spread out a distribution is, so the extra term rewards the LM for not collapsing onto a few responses (not from the inputs).

## What this says about RLHF

11. This is the paper's justification of RLHF: setting γ to zero in RKL gives exactly the RLHF objective, so RLHF is reverse KL minimization in that limit, and RKL is RLHF with "a minor correction". The guarantee in step 13 has a factor 1/γ², so it does not cover γ = 0 itself. ([§5, key point 3](yun2025/sections/reverse-kl.md#key-point-3); [Theorem 7](yun2025/theorems/theorem-7.md))

12. The correction matters, the paper argues, because of the dilemma in step 2: without the entropy term, RLHF either stays near the reference LM or collapses. No theorem states this. One synthetic experiment, with data generated from the paper's own assumption, shows RLHF's error leveling off above zero while RKL's falls toward zero as the data grow. ([§5, key point 4](yun2025/sections/reverse-kl.md#key-point-4); [Figure 1](yun2025/figures-and-tables/figure-1.md))

13. RKL's guarantee is where the three methods separate. Its error is at most of order ln|Π|/n + C_𝓡·ln|𝓡| / (γ²·n). The coverage coefficient is now that of the reward models, C_𝓡, not of the whole candidate class, and the paper says these terms "may both be much smaller" than PMLE's. Its explanation: RKL uses the collected response pairs only to train the reward model, and trains the LM on its own responses. (§5, key points [5](yun2025/sections/reverse-kl.md#key-point-5) and [6](yun2025/sections/reverse-kl.md#key-point-6); [Theorem 7](yun2025/theorems/theorem-7.md))

14. From this the paper draws its broadest claim: that the theory confirms, for the first time, the empirical finding that RLHF-style training outperforms DPO-style training. The Abstract says the theory "explains" the finding; the Introduction says it "can be seen as" a confirmation. What supports it is a comparison of two upper bounds, under the extra assumption of step 9. ([Introduction, key point 7](yun2025/sections/introduction.md#key-point-7); [Theorem 7](yun2025/theorems/theorem-7.md))

## What was tested

Conditions for the results below ([Experiments](yun2025/sections/experiments.md); [Appendix D, key point 7](yun2025/sections/appendix-d-experimental-details.md#key-point-7)): the experiments ask whether the three objectives are competitive with the methods they resemble. They do not test the 1/n rates or the ordering of step 13. Every method is trained with the penalty toward the reference LM switched on, which the guarantees set to zero. Quality is judged by LLMs, not by people.

15. On summarization, with two model sizes and three seeds, each proposed objective has a higher win-rate than its baseline in all six comparisons, by 1.0 to 3.4 points; in three of the six the spreads overlap. On the reward-model score, which the paper does not discuss, the baseline is higher in two of six. (Experiments, key points [2](yun2025/sections/experiments.md#key-point-2) and [3](yun2025/sections/experiments.md#key-point-3); [Table 2](yun2025/figures-and-tables/table-2.md))

16. On general chat, with one model, PMLE and preference distillation score higher than DPO and REBEL in seven of eight comparisons. The values are single runs with no spread, and RKL was not run. (Experiments, key points [6](yun2025/sections/experiments.md#key-point-6) and [7](yun2025/sections/experiments.md#key-point-7); [Table 3](yun2025/figures-and-tables/table-3.md))

17. The paper's reading of both is that the objectives are "competitive with strong baselines" and "promising". (Experiments, key points [5](yun2025/sections/experiments.md#key-point-5) and [8](yun2025/sections/experiments.md#key-point-8))

## What the paper concludes

18. One explicit assumption about preferences yields three methods that "correct and generalize" DPO, REBEL and RLHF, each with an error guarantee of order 1/n, and that are competitive in practice. As future work it names the exponential dependence of its constants on R, other divergences and preference models, and comparing mode-seeking with mode-covering objectives. It has no limitations section. (Conclusion, key points [1](yun2025/sections/conclusion.md#key-point-1) to [3](yun2025/sections/conclusion.md#key-point-3))
