# Logical chain: Beyond RLHF: A Unified Theoretical Framework of Alignment (yun2025)

Theorems, equations, tables and figures are named by the numbers the compiled paper prints, worked out from the LaTeX source.

## 1 Introduction, with the abstract

1. <a id="step-1"></a>The paper starts from the method it goes on to question. Alignment is the task of improving an LLM's responses, such as their helpfulness and harmlessness, from human preferences, and the paper calls it the de facto final step of LLM training ([§1 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s1-p1)). RLHF, the first method for it, trains a reward model $\hat R$ from pairwise preferences and then trains the language model, the policy $\pi$, by reinforcement learning to maximize the learned reward of its own responses minus $\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\,\Vert\,\pi_0)$, a penalty on its divergence from a supervised fine-tuned reference model $\pi_0$ ([Eq. 1](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:rlhf)).

2. <a id="step-2"></a>The paper questions this objective because the field treats it as the goal of alignment itself, which it shows with three observations ([§1 ¶2](source/yun2025/paper.md#s1-p2)). DPO can be viewed as RLHF rewritten under a strong "all-policy" assumption into likelihood-ratio terms ([Eq. 6](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:dpo)), so that it does not rely on on-policy responses, that is, responses sampled from the policy being trained; ΨPO replaces the reward with a transformation of the preference probability; and theoretical analyses "often" take the RLHF objective or a variant of it as the learning goal and prove guarantees such as regret bounds for it. The paper grants that the objective "has proven useful" and that "one may argue that it is a sensible objective".

   The paper expands DPO as "Direct Policy Optimization" here and as "Direct Preference Optimization", the title of the cited work, in its related-work appendix ([§A, Without RL and without a reward model ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sA-without-rl-and-without-a-reward-model-p1), [reference rafailov23direct](source/yun2025/paper.md#ref-rafailov23direct)). It uses the term "all-policy assumption" three times, each time citing the DPO paper, and defines it nowhere; it gives no derivation of DPO from RLHF. Its fullest statement is in its description of REBEL ([step 23](#step-23)), as motivated by "the characterization of the RLHF solution under the all-policy assumption" ([§4, Preference distillation ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s4-preference-distillation-p1)).

3. <a id="step-3"></a>Against that standing, the paper claims that the RLHF objective "has no known justification from a learning-theoretic viewpoint", and argues it in three points; none is a formal result ([§1 ¶3](source/yun2025/paper.md#s1-p3)).

   - Existing guarantees typically show convergence to "the solution of the population RLHF objective with the true reward", which the paper calls a tautology that "can hardly be interpreted as a justification" and later restates as "convergence to the population version of the training objective" ([§1, Our contributions ¶3](source/yun2025/paper.md#s1-our-contributions-p3)). It defines neither "population" nor "the true reward" here. Elsewhere it says only that RLHF's Bradley–Terry model depends on "some reward function" ([§1, Our contributions ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s1-our-contributions-p1)), that RLHF pipelines fit a reward model "to approximate the underlying true reward $R^*$" ([step 27](#step-27)), and that earlier studies do not justify "why a certain reward construction must be optimized in the first place" ([step 14](#step-14)).
   - Maximizing reward "informally sounds reasonable", but the problem definition of alignment "does not involve rewards at all", so it is unclear what it formally means.
   - The paper says that the "a priori" dependence on reward "manifests" in the following issue. Eq. 1 is a loss plus a regularizer, so with enough data $\beta$ should shrink, and in the asymptotic regime become zero "to ensure sufficient learning". Asymptotically, then, the objective faces a dilemma: if $\beta$ goes to zero, the maximizer of Eq. 1 becomes deterministic, which is undesirable for a language model; if it stays above zero, the regularizer hinders learning. The appendix gives the maximizer in closed form for a single categorical distribution: the reference distribution reweighted by $\exp(\text{reward}/\text{regularization strength})$ ([§B, Prior smoothing ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sB-prior-smoothing-p1); [step 8](#step-8)); the paper does not work out the limit.

   From the third point the paper concludes that this "suggests that, in principle, RLHF requires some correction". The correction is in [step 7](#step-7), and the toy experiment on the dilemma in [step 9](#step-9).

4. <a id="step-4"></a>Having set the existing guarantees aside, the paper names one strong form of justification: a provable guarantee such as statistical convergence to the target language model, the model that alignment is meant to produce ([§1 ¶4](source/yun2025/paper.md#s1-p4)). Existing analyses are "typically only applicable to their method of choice": the frameworks for DPO and those for RLHF "have different theoretical targets", so their guarantees cannot be compared directly. The introduction does not say what the targets are. The related-work appendix describes the RLHF-side analyses as giving regret bounds or guarantees on policy value ([§A, Theoretical analyses of preference optimization ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sA-theoretical-analyses-of-preference-optimization-p1)), and the DPO-side analysis of Agarwal et al. as bounding a forward KL divergence to "an optimal policy" ([§A, Theoretical analyses of preference optimization ¶2](source/yun2025/paper.md#sA-theoretical-analyses-of-preference-optimization-p2)); it does not say for which objective that policy is optimal, and it does not describe the other DPO-side analysis it cites ([reference kveton25active](source/yun2025/paper.md#ref-kveton25active)).

   The analyses that step 2 says "often" take the RLHF objective or a variant as their goal are the five works cited here for the RLHF side; the DPO side is two other works. The paper does not say how the two statements hold together, that is, whether the DPO-side targets count among the "variants" of step 2. Its appendix describes Agarwal et al. as analysing "offline RLHF variants that minimize DPO-type objectives" ([§A, Theoretical analyses of preference optimization ¶2](source/yun2025/paper.md#sA-theoretical-analyses-of-preference-optimization-p2)), and describes two of the five RLHF-side works as versions of DPO, an "exploratory version of DPO" and χPO, which it also lists among the variants of DPO, and a third as an "online direct alignment algorithm" ([§A, Theoretical analyses of preference optimization ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sA-theoretical-analyses-of-preference-optimization-p1), [§A, Without RL and without a reward model ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sA-without-rl-and-without-a-reward-model-p1)).

5. <a id="step-5"></a>The two gaps, no justification and no common target, lead to the paper's question: under what assumptions, for instance on how the target LM is related to the preference data, can existing training objectives be justified or new ones developed ([§1 ¶5](source/yun2025/paper.md#s1-p5))? Its way of answering is a recipe it calls popular in learning theory, the one behind logistic regression, latent Dirichlet allocation and the variational autoencoder: posit a target model, assume how the data is generated from it, and derive objectives from statistical principles, which makes the assumptions explicit and gives guarantees such as consistency, convergence to the target as the sample grows ([§1 ¶6](source/yun2025/paper.md#s1-p6)). The recipe is not new. The paper's own claim is applying it to alignment, where it says such a "fully probabilistic" framework "remains largely unexplored".

6. <a id="step-6"></a>The assumption the paper puts into the recipe is that there is a target ("oracle") LM $\pi^*$, and that for a prompt $x$ response $a$ is preferred to $b$ with probability

   $$\mathbb{P}(a\succ b\mid x)=\frac{\pi^*(a\mid x)^\gamma}{\pi^*(a\mid x)^\gamma+\pi^*(b\mid x)^\gamma}\qquad\text{for some }\gamma>0$$

   ([§1, Our contributions ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s1-our-contributions-p1), [Eq. 2](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:preference-model-intro)). The intuition given is that $\pi^*$ "must assign a higher probability to the preferred response". The exponent $\gamma$ sets how far differences in response probability are accentuated or attenuated; in practice it is a hyperparameter, typically between 0 and 1 ([§2, Our preference model ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s2-our-preference-model-p1)). The form is a Bradley–Terry model, which is not new and which RLHF also uses with a score that depends on a reward; the paper's own part is the score, the target LM's probability raised to $\gamma$, so that the preference model "is explicitly a language model". The paper contrasts this with DPO, which reaches a relationship between the reward model and the LM only through the all-policy assumption, which the paper calls unrealistic without giving a reason. Eq. 2 is posited here; the paper's defence of it is in [step 14](#step-14).

7. <a id="step-7"></a>From this assumption the paper derives three training objectives, each set beside an existing method ([§1, Our contributions ¶2](source/yun2025/paper.md#s1-our-contributions-p2), [Table 1](source/yun2025/paper.md#tab:tasks)). It ties the resemblance to its question of step 5: the derived objectives "often resemble baselines (DPO, RLHF, REBEL)", so that it is "essentially making the hidden assumptions of existing methods explicit" ([§2, Is our preference model too strong? ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s2-is-our-preference-model-too-strong-p1)).

   - **PMLE** (preference maximum likelihood estimate), beside DPO: the likelihood of the observed preferences under Eq. 2, with a KL regularizer toward $\pi_0$; no reward model and no RL. The difference from DPO that the paper states is that DPO has no explicit regularizer (steps 17 and 18).
   - **Preference distillation**, beside REBEL: a candidate LM is trained to match, on the response pairs of the data, the preference that a learned reward model gives; a reward model and no RL. The difference the paper states from existing reward distillation is that this objective is derived from Eq. 2 (step 23).
   - **Reverse KL (RKL)**, beside RLHF. The goal being to learn a distribution, the paper calls it natural to minimize the reverse KL $\mathbb{E}_x[\mathrm{KL}(\hat\pi(x)\,\Vert\,\pi^*(x))]$, which needs the unknown $\pi^*$; under Eq. 2 a reward model fitted to the preferences estimates $\gamma\ln\pi^*$ up to a constant ([step 22](#step-22)). Putting it in gives the RLHF objective with one more term, the policy's entropy with weight $\gamma$ ([Eq. 15](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:rkl)); a reward model and RL. The paper says the result "can be seen as a correction to RLHF" (step 27). On the dilemma of step 3: without the regularizer toward $\pi_0$ the RKL objective is, up to constants, the reverse KL from the model the learned reward induces, a distribution over all responses that estimates $\pi^*$, and not a single response for each prompt ([§C.4 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sC.4-p1)).

8. <a id="step-8"></a>For each objective the paper proves a guarantee of the form step 4 named: an upper bound $\mathbb{E}_x[\mathrm{KL}(\pi^*(x)\,\Vert\,\hat\pi(x))]\le O(1/n)$ on the forward KL error, $n$ being the number of preference pairs ([§1, Our contributions ¶2](source/yun2025/paper.md#s1-our-contributions-p2), [Table 1](source/yun2025/paper.md#tab:tasks)). The forward KL has the target first, the reverse KL of step 7 the learned model first; RKL's bound is also on the forward KL, for reasons given in [step 31](#step-31). The abstract adds that the objectives thereby are "naturally avoiding degeneracy" ([Abstract ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#abstract-p1)) and does not say what degeneracy means. The paper uses "degenerate" in three places: of DPO, which another work proves "may converge to a degenerate distribution" ([§3, Remark ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s3-remark-p1)); of RLHF as $\beta$ goes to zero ([§5, The dilemma of RLHF ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s5-the-dilemma-of-rlhf-p1)); and of RKL in the appendix's single-categorical example, where RKL's maximizer differs from that of RLHF with its regularization strength written as $\beta+\gamma$ only by an exponent $\beta/(\beta+\gamma)$, below one, on the reference model's probabilities, which smooths them so that the estimate "would not be too close to a degenerate distribution" even if the reference model is ([§B, Prior smoothing ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sB-prior-smoothing-p1)).

   The bounds are non-asymptotic, holding at each finite $n$, and the paper calls them the first of their kind for learning a distribution from pairwise feedback "to the best of our knowledge" ([§1, Our contributions ¶3](source/yun2025/paper.md#s1-our-contributions-p3)). Beside the claim it cites with "cf." a work it does not describe ([reference dumoulin23density](source/yun2025/paper.md#ref-dumoulin23density)). The forward KL bound of Agarwal et al. in step 4 is set aside in two ways: the paper calls it "merely a byproduct of their strong realizability assumption" and says it gives the rate $1/\sqrt{n}$ in this framework, against the paper's $1/n$ ([§A, Theoretical analyses of preference optimization ¶2](source/yun2025/paper.md#sA-theoretical-analyses-of-preference-optimization-p2); [step 19](#step-19)).

   The $O(1/n)$ leaves out conditions stated later. The theorems are for the objectives without the regularizer toward $\pi_0$, $\beta=0$ ([step 20](#step-20)); they assume a finite model class containing $\pi^*$ and a bound $R$ on the models' centered log-probabilities ([Assumption 1](source/yun2025/paper.md#assp:real), [Assumption 2](source/yun2025/paper.md#assp:bdd); [step 15](#step-15)), and for preference distillation and RKL also that the LMs induced by the reward models lie in the policy class ([Assumption 5](source/yun2025/paper.md#assp:reward_model); [step 24](#step-24)). The bounds carry the logarithm of a class size and a coverage coefficient ([Definition 3](source/yun2025/paper.md#def:cov); [step 16](#step-16)), and hide factors in $R$ ([§3, Convergence guarantee ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s3-convergence-guarantee-p1)), whose dependence the conclusion calls exponential ([§7 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s7-p1)).

Conditions of the toy experiment behind Figure 1 ([§D.1 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sD.1-p1), [§5, The dilemma of RLHF ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s5-the-dilemma-of-rlhf-p1)): a tabular problem with 10 prompts and 10 responses; preferences generated from Eq. 2 with $\gamma=0.5$; $\pi^*$ and $\pi_0$ drawn at random and kept close; RLHF (Eq. 1) against RKL (Eq. 15), each with the best $\beta$ "from a wide range" at each sample size $n$ from $2^5$ to $2^{16}$; the measure is the forward KL to $\pi^*$.

9. <a id="step-9"></a>The paper checks the dilemma of step 3 and the correction of step 7 on this toy problem ([Figure 1](source/yun2025/paper.md#fig:rlhf_comparison), [§1, Our contributions ¶2](source/yun2025/paper.md#s1-our-contributions-p2)). In the figure both curves start at about 0.63 to 0.65; the RKL curve falls to about 0.01 at $n=2^{16}$, and the RLHF curve levels off near 0.4 from about $n=2^{12}$. The paper reads this as showing that "under our preference model" RLHF with $\beta$ tuned for each $n$ is inconsistent, does not converge to $\pi^*$, whereas RKL is consistent; Section 5 says RLHF's forward KL "eventually saturates at a non-zero value" ([§5, The dilemma of RLHF ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s5-the-dilemma-of-rlhf-p1)). The figure shows one curve per method with no spread. The paper does not state how many runs a curve is from, the range of $\beta$ or the criterion for the best one, how the learned reward was obtained in the toy, the forward KL of $\pi_0$ itself, or the $\beta$ chosen at each $n$, so the figure does not show how either tuned policy compares with $\pi_0$, which the conditions say is close to $\pi^*$.

10. <a id="step-10"></a>Since the three bounds of step 8 are on the same quantity, they can be compared, which step 4 said existing theory does not allow. The introduction makes one comparison: the guarantee shows that RKL has a "superior" guarantee to PMLE ([§1, Our contributions ¶3](source/yun2025/paper.md#s1-our-contributions-p3)). The difference, given in Section 5, is the coverage coefficient: PMLE's bound has that of the whole policy class, RKL's that of the smaller class of LMs induced by the reward models, so that under Assumption 5 RKL's terms "may both be much smaller" ([step 31](#step-31)). As RKL is similar to RLHF and PMLE to DPO, the paper says this "can be seen as" the first theoretical confirmation of "the common empirical finding that RLHF outperforms DPO"; the abstract says the theory "explains" the finding that on-policy objectives "typically outperform" likelihood-style ones ([Abstract ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#abstract-p1)).

    The support is a comparison of two upper bounds, one for RKL and one for PMLE. The paper proves no bound for RLHF or DPO themselves, and no lower bound showing that PMLE's error cannot be as small as RKL's. It gives no source for the empirical finding where it states it; the related-work appendix says that direct optimization from preference labels "has been noted to underperform along some dimensions compared to conventional RLHF" ([§A, Without RL and without a reward model ¶2](source/yun2025/paper.md#sA-without-rl-and-without-a-reward-model-p2)), and the paragraph before it says that DPO "was shown to match PPO-based RLHF" ([§A, Without RL and without a reward model ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sA-without-rl-and-without-a-reward-model-p1)). The paper does not say how this fits the finding. Step 9 says the RLHF objective as it stands does not converge to $\pi^*$ in the toy; the two hold together because the grounding the paper offers for RLHF is "up to a minor correction", the entropy term of step 7 ([§1, Our contributions ¶3](source/yun2025/paper.md#s1-our-contributions-p3)).

11. <a id="step-11"></a>The last part of the argument is empirical: whether the derived objectives also perform in practice ([§1, Our contributions ¶4](source/yun2025/paper.md#s1-our-contributions-p4)). The introduction says the methods "generally outperform baseline win-rates in TL;DR summarization and generate more preferred responses in general chat"; the abstract says "empirical results indicate" that they are "competitive with strong baselines across several tasks and models" ([Abstract ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#abstract-p1)). There are two experiments: summarization, with all three methods, judged by GPT-4 against human reference summaries ([§6.1 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s6.1-p1)), and general chat, with PMLE and preference distillation only, judged by an LLM on three benchmarks ([§6.2 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s6.2-p1)). The results and which wording they bear out are in steps 32 to 36.

## 2 Preliminaries

12. <a id="step-12"></a>Section 2 makes the recipe of step 5 a formal problem ([§2, Alignment as distribution learning ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s2-alignment-as-distribution-learning-p1)). There is a space $\mathcal{X}$ of prompts with a fixed distribution $\mathcal{D}$, and a space $\mathcal{A}$ of responses; an LM is a policy $\pi$ giving a distribution $\pi(\cdot\mid x)$ over responses for each prompt. Alignment is learning the target $\pi^*$ from preference feedback drawn from a model that depends on it: given a class $\Pi$ of LMs, find $\hat\pi\in\Pi$ "as close as possible" to $\pi^*$ under "a suitable measure of distance between distributions". The measure is not fixed here; the theorems use the forward KL of step 8.

13. <a id="step-13"></a>The second ingredient of the recipe is how the data is generated ([§2, Our preference model ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s2-our-preference-model-p1)). The responses come not from $\pi^*$ but from another LM $\mu$, which "could be a reference LLM or simply an existing dataset". The paper does not say whether this reference LLM is the $\pi_0$ of step 1, which it describes in the same words; in one case of Section 4 the two are different models ([step 25](#step-25)). The dataset $D_n$ holds $n$ independent triples $(x,a^+,a^-)$: two responses drawn from $\mu$, ordered by a preference drawn from Eq. 2. The paper writes the preference probability $\mathbb{P}_\pi$ for any LM $\pi$ in place of $\pi^*$ ([Eq. 3](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:preference-model)), which gives every candidate model a probability for each observed preference, the material of Section 3. Candidates are scored with the same $\gamma$ that generates the data; the paper, which treats $\gamma$ as a hyperparameter, does not say how that $\gamma$ would be known or what holds when the training $\gamma$ differs.

14. <a id="step-14"></a>The paper then defends the assumption of step 6 against the objection that it is too restrictive: "we claim that it is not" ([§2, Is our preference model too strong? ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s2-is-our-preference-model-too-strong-p1)). It splits it in two. (i) The Bradley–Terry form: the paper grants drawbacks "such as not allowing cyclic preferences" and defends it as "conventional in many prior works"; it says its perspective "can be extended to incorporate general preference models", gives no such extension, and lists it as future work ([§7 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s7-p1)). (ii) The link of the preference score to $\pi^*$, which it calls "the crucial link" for guarantees; its argument is that earlier theory proves convergence for reward-maximizing objectives "without justifying why a certain reward construction must be optimized in the first place", whereas this paper identifies the assumptions under which they are justified, and its objectives "often resemble" DPO, RLHF and REBEL. The assumption rests on this argument. The paper does not test whether preference data follows Eq. 2: the toy data of step 9 is generated from Eq. 2, and the experiments of Section 6 measure the quality of trained models.

15. <a id="step-15"></a>For the guarantees the paper fixes the terms of a model's error and two assumptions ([§2, Theoretical setup ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s2-theoretical-setup-p1)). It calls $R_\pi=\gamma\ln\pi$ the reward induced by $\pi$ (a footnote says there is no actual reward in PMLE), writes $\bar R_\pi$ for it centered by its mean over responses from $\mu$, and $\Delta\bar R_\pi=\bar R_\pi-\bar R_{\pi^*}$ for a model's difference from the target. A model enters Eq. 3 only through differences of $R_\pi$ at two responses, which the centering leaves unchanged. This is the quantity the proofs work with: the data bounds the mean of $\Delta\bar R_{\hat\pi}^2$ over responses from $\mu$ ([Eq. 17](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:delta)), and the forward KL is bounded by its mean over responses from $\pi^*$ ([§C.2 ¶4](source/yun2025/paper.md#sC.2-p4), [Eq. 19](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:novelty)); the passage between the two is [step 16](#step-16). The paper calls its two assumptions standard in the alignment literature:

    - Realizability ([Assumption 1](source/yun2025/paper.md#assp:real)): $\pi^*$ is in $\Pi$, and $\Pi$ is finite. Among the works cited for "standard" is Agarwal et al., whose "strong realizability assumption (Assumption 3.2)" the appendix holds against their bound (step 8); the paper does not say what that assumption states or how it differs from Assumption 1. Its proof appendix describes their theorem as relying on the population loss of $\hat\pi$ being not too far from that of $\pi^*$, "which is rather strong" ([§C.2 ¶3](source/yun2025/paper.md#sC.2-p3)), without saying that this is their Assumption 3.2.
    - Boundedness ([Assumption 2](source/yun2025/paper.md#assp:bdd)): $|\bar R_\pi|\le\gamma R$ for every model of $\Pi$, which in the proofs keeps the ratio of two models' probabilities for a response within $e^{4R}$ ([Eq. 18](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:prob_ratio)).

16. <a id="step-16"></a>A further condition is needed because the data comes from $\mu$ and the guarantee is about $\pi^*$, an offline problem with distribution shift ([§2, Theoretical setup ¶2](source/yun2025/paper.md#s2-theoretical-setup-p2)). The paper says a coverage assumption is therefore necessary, as in offline RL, and uses a "generalized coverage coefficient" cited to Xie et al. (2021) and Agarwal et al. (2025), claiming nothing in it as new ([Definition 3](source/yun2025/paper.md#def:cov)). For a class of models, the coefficient is the factor by which a model's squared error $\Delta\bar R^2$ on responses from $\mu$, where the data is, can understate its squared error on responses from $\pi^*$, where the guarantee is needed. It is a definition: no value is assumed, and it appears as a factor in the bounds, for the policy class $\Pi$ and for the class of LMs induced by the reward models.

    The paper says the coefficient "improves upon" the all-policy concentrability condition of Munos (2003), a bound on $\pi/\mu$ over every model, prompt and response, since it "can be bounded even if the latter is infinite", "depending on" the prompt distribution and the class of reward differences ([§2, Theoretical setup ¶3](source/yun2025/paper.md#s2-theoretical-setup-p3)); it gives no argument or example for this. It does not say whether the "all-policy assumption" of step 2 is this condition; it cites the two to different works. A footnote says pessimism "may further improve" the coefficient to one that "relies on $\pi^*$ rather than the policy class", beyond the paper's scope; the paper explains neither pessimism nor that coefficient.

## 3 Preference Maximum Likelihood Estimation Approach

17. <a id="step-17"></a>Section 3 derives the first objective by maximum likelihood ([§3 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s3-p1)). Under Eq. 3 the probability of a recorded preference is a sigmoid of $\gamma$ times the log-ratio of the candidate's probabilities for the preferred and the dispreferred response, and the PMLE loss is the average negative log of it over the data ([Eq. 4](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:mle-nokl)). For a reason of practice, that what is optimized is typically a fine-tuned model $\pi_0$, the paper calls it "natural" to add the KL penalty toward $\pi_0$ of the RLHF objective, which gives the PMLE objective ([Eq. 5](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:mle-objective)). It keeps the two apart afterwards: Eq. 4 is "the objective derived from purely considering preference feedback" ([§3, Convergence guarantee ¶3](source/yun2025/paper.md#s3-convergence-guarantee-p3)), and Table 1 gives Eq. 4, not Eq. 5, as PMLE's objective.

    The two parts use different responses: the likelihood the dataset's, the penalty the policy's own, which the summarization implementation computes on "online responses" following Song et al. ([§D.2, Implementations ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sD.2-implementations-p1)), whose method HyPO the appendix describes in the same way ([§A, Without RL and without a reward model ¶2](source/yun2025/paper.md#sA-without-rl-and-without-a-reward-model-p2)). Table 1 lists PMLE as requiring no RL training, and the introduction calls it, "similarly to DPO", "relatively straightforward to optimize" ([§1, Our contributions ¶2](source/yun2025/paper.md#s1-our-contributions-p2)); the paper does not say how this fits a penalty computed on responses from the policy. Its appendix groups PMLE with DPO as "trained on the preference dataset" against the methods that "utilize the online responses" ([§D.2, Dataset ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sD.2-dataset-p1)). Its reported training time for PMLE is about 6 hours, against 3 for DPO and about 2.5 days for the four methods with a reward model, all on 4 A100 GPUs ([§D.2, Implementations ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sD.2-implementations-p1)).

18. <a id="step-18"></a>The paper compares the objective with DPO ([§3, Remark ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s3-remark-p1), [Eq. 6](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:dpo)). They differ in two places: DPO subtracts the reference model's log-ratio inside the sigmoid, and PMLE adds the KL penalty outside it. The paper names only the second, that DPO "does not have an explicit regularizer". It writes DPO's coefficient with its own symbol $\gamma$ without saying what it is in DPO; its hyperparameter tables list for DPO a "$\beta$" of 0.05 and no $\gamma$ ([Table 5](source/yun2025/paper.md#tab:tldr_hparams), [Table 6](source/yun2025/paper.md#tab:chat_hparams)).

    The paper says the missing regularizer "could lead to undesirable behaviors if the policy class $\Pi$ is sufficiently expressive", with two cited results and one argument. Fisch et al. "prove that DPO may converge to a degenerate distribution" (the paper does not say which; step 8). Song et al. "show that DPO relies on a strong coverage assumption": if $\pi_0$ "does not fully cover the relevant distribution", DPO can produce out-of-distribution responses, "making its reward estimates inaccurate". The argument is the contrast with RLHF, whose KL term keeps the policy within the support of $\pi_0$, where DPO can give probability to responses that $\pi_0$ "would never select", "undermining performance guarantees". The paper does not say why expressiveness matters, which distribution is "the relevant" one, which guarantees are meant, or what reward DPO, which has no reward model, estimates; its nearest statement is the introduction's, that DPO finds a relationship between the reward model and the LM (step 6). PMLE's own bound also depends on coverage ([step 19](#step-19)); the paper does not compare DPO's coverage assumption with it, and the remark speaks of coverage by $\pi_0$, Definition 3 of coverage by $\mu$. The paper concludes that PMLE's explicit KL term "effectively circumvents the aforementioned pitfalls".

    This conclusion is argued from the analogy with RLHF's KL term; nothing is proved for the objective with the penalty, since the theorem is for $\beta=0$ ([step 20](#step-20)). The experiments report PMLE's divergence from $\pi_0$ as less than half DPO's in summarization ([step 34](#step-34)); in general chat PMLE is run with the KL coefficient at 0.0 ([Table 6](source/yun2025/paper.md#tab:chat_hparams)).

19. <a id="step-19"></a>The guarantee announced in step 8 is Theorem 4: the minimizer $\hat\pi$ over $\Pi$ of Eq. 4, without the penalty, has, with probability at least $1-\delta$, forward KL error $\lesssim\frac{C_\Pi}{\gamma^2}\cdot\frac{\ln(|\Pi|/\delta)}{n}$, where $C_\Pi$ is the coverage coefficient of the policy class and $\lesssim$ hides a factor the paper says depends only on $R$ ([Theorem 4](source/yun2025/paper.md#thm:mle), [Eq. 7](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:pmle_guarantee)). The proof runs from the likelihood to preference probabilities by a maximum-likelihood bound the paper calls "mostly classical", following Foster and Krishnamurthy ([Proposition 12](source/yun2025/paper.md#thm:oracle), [§C.2 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sC.2-p1)), then to centered rewards ([Lemma 8](source/yun2025/paper.md#lem:sig_diff), [Eq. 17](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:delta)), then by Definition 3 to responses from $\pi^*$, and finally to the forward KL ([Eq. 19](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:novelty)) ([§C.2 ¶4](source/yun2025/paper.md#sC.2-p4)). One inequality it uses is not proved in the paper but referred to Foster and Krishnamurthy ([Lemma 11](source/yun2025/paper.md#thm:symmetrization), [§C.1 ¶8](source/yun2025/paper.md#sC.1-p8)).

    The paper claims the last move, Eq. 19, as "one of our key novelties" ([Remark 13](source/yun2025/paper.md#rem:novelty)): Schulman's trick, which it cites to a web page, followed by Lemma 9, one of the "basic results" of the appendix, bounds the forward KL by the mean squared log-ratio, where Agarwal et al., in the paper's account, use the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, so that following their approach "naively" would turn $1/n$ into $1/\sqrt{n}$. This is the difference in rate of step 8. The proof is "inspired in part by" theirs ([§3, Convergence guarantee ¶2](source/yun2025/paper.md#s3-convergence-guarantee-p2)); "the key difference", the paper says, is that their theorem relies on the population loss of $\hat\pi$ being close to that of $\pi^*$, whereas its own "provides an end-to-end guarantee" ([§C.2 ¶3](source/yun2025/paper.md#sC.2-p3)). It does not define "end-to-end" or say which part of its proof takes the place of that assumption.

    The hidden factor, in the last display of the proof ([§C.2 ¶4](source/yun2025/paper.md#sC.2-p4)), grows exponentially in $R$ ([§7 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s7-p1)), and it contains $e^{4\gamma R}$, which depends on $\gamma$, where the text says the hidden constants rely only on $R$. That factor enters where Lemma 8 is applied to the preference probabilities, whose arguments are bounded by $2\gamma R$ (Eq. 17), and is separate from the $e^{4R}$ ratio bound of step 15. The paper gives no value of $R$, $C_\Pi$ or $|\Pi|$ for any class of language models.

20. <a id="step-20"></a>The theorem covers less than the objective of step 17: it is for $\beta=0$, as is every analysis in the paper, for simplicity and to show that the preference loss alone "already suffices to learn the true distribution $\pi^*$" ([§3, Convergence guarantee ¶3](source/yun2025/paper.md#s3-convergence-guarantee-p3)). The paper states the dilemma of step 3 for RLHF; for PMLE the theorem covers $\beta=0$, where the minimizer converges to $\pi^*$, and the paper does not say whether, for $\beta>0$, the penalty of Eq. 5 hinders learning as it says RLHF's does.

    The guarantee and the remark of step 18 are therefore about different objectives: the guarantee for Eq. 4 under Assumptions 1 and 2, the advantage over DPO for Eq. 5 with $\beta>0$, where nothing is proved. The theorem is for a finite class satisfying Assumption 2 and the behaviours of DPO for a "sufficiently expressive" class; the paper does not relate the two, and so does not say whether those behaviours can occur for Eq. 4, which has no explicit regularizer either. On the penalty it "posit[s]" that starting from a well-aligned $\pi_0$ "can result in improved guarantees by mitigating the dependency of constants on $R$", and leaves this to future work, as the conclusion does ([§7 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s7-p1)).

## 4 Preference Distillation Approach

21. <a id="step-21"></a>The paper turns to the two objectives that use a reward model, to be read as derived "from a distribution learning perspective rather than reward maximization" ([§3, Convergence guarantee ¶4](source/yun2025/paper.md#s3-convergence-guarantee-p4)). Section 4 starts from existing work ([§4 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s4-p1)): where RLHF uses the reward model to make alignment an RL problem, recent studies, citing Guo et al. (of whom the paper gives the citation only) and Fisch et al., "have attempted" to use it in supervised losses, "objectives that do not require RL to solve", efforts that "can be seen as" distilling information from the reward model, as Fisch et al. point out. The paper asserts one benefit, avoiding RL algorithms, "which are typically slow to converge", and one cost, training the reward model, which it calls "typically quite low" because a shallow network on an existing LLM's frozen layers "usually suffices". It reports no training time for a reward model and does not say whether its reported times include one; the summarization times do not separate the methods with RL from those without, and the general chat time is not assigned to methods ([§D.2, Implementations ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sD.2-implementations-p1), [§D.3, Implementation ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sD.3-implementation-p1), [Table 5](source/yun2025/paper.md#tab:tldr_hparams)).

22. <a id="step-22"></a>A reward model can enter a framework without rewards because under the preference model it is an estimate of the target LM ([§4, Reward model ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s4-reward-model-p1)): learning a reward model "is equivalent to" learning an LM $\pi$ with $R=\gamma\ln\pi$ up to a constant, and conversely a reward model $R$ gives the LM $\pi\propto\exp(R/\gamma)$ ([Eq. 8](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:policy_from_reward_model)). Fitting a reward model to the preferences ([Eq. 9](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:reward-model-training)) is then, in the paper's words, "equivalent to the PMLE objective under Eq. 8 but with the constraint $R\in\mathcal{R}$", since the normalizer cancels in the ratio of two responses' probabilities. The paper writes $\tilde\pi\propto\exp(\hat R/\gamma)$ for the LM induced by the fitted reward. Sampling from such an LM "is computationally intractable in general", and the paper calls it "non-generative"; Section 4 gives no reason, and Appendix B gives one, that only the unnormalized form is available ([§B, Intractability of forward KL ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sB-intractability-of-forward-kl-p1)). Fitting a reward model to pairwise preferences is the first stage of RLHF; the paper's own part is to read the fitted model as an estimate of $\pi^*$, which rests on Eq. 3.

23. <a id="step-23"></a>With the reward model read as an estimate of $\pi^*$, the paper turns to the existing use of a reward model without RL, distillation, and builds its objective against REBEL, which it calls "one popular method for distilling rewards" and cites to Gao et al., a work other than the Guo et al. of step 21 ([§4, Preference distillation ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s4-preference-distillation-p1), [reference gao24rebel](source/yun2025/paper.md#ref-gao24rebel)). REBEL regresses, with a squared loss, the difference of the policy's log-ratios to $\pi_0$ at the two responses on $\eta$ times the difference of their learned rewards ([Eq. 10](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:rebel)). The paper writes the loss over the preference dataset; its appendix says REBEL's regression "is repeated with batches of on-policy responses" and that Fisch et al.'s distillation is a version using only the dataset's responses ([§A, Without RL but with a reward model ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sA-without-rl-but-with-a-reward-model-p1)), and REBEL's code samples online responses ([§D.2, Pseudocode ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sD.2-pseudocode-p1)). The paper does not say whether Eq. 10 as written is REBEL as it is run. Under Eq. 3 the paper says "we could" drop the $\pi_0$ terms and set $\eta=1/\gamma$, and does not, because the squared loss "is not well justified from a statistical perspective" and it is "unclear" whether it should be preferred to, say, an absolute loss. This is asserted; the paper has no result on the squared loss, and its experiments compare the methods as wholes.

    Its replacement ([§4, Preference distillation ¶2](source/yun2025/paper.md#s4-preference-distillation-p2)): the fitted model is a "preference simulator" that can label any pair of responses through Eq. 3, and using such labels in PMLE "would yield a natural distribution learning objective". Drawing labels adds randomness "which can hinder optimization", which is asserted with no argument or experiment, so the paper uses the expected label, the simulator's preference probability $\sigma(\hat R(x,a^+)-\hat R(x,a^-))$. The log-loss against it is, up to a constant, the KL divergence between the simulator's and the candidate's preference distributions, averaged over the pairs ([Eq. 11](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:distill)); "in practice" the KL penalty toward $\pi_0$ is added ([Eq. 12](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:pref_distill)). Against REBEL the objective has a different error measure and no $\pi_0$ term in the loss; the difference the paper names is that it "is explicitly derived from" Eq. 2 ([§1, Our contributions ¶2](source/yun2025/paper.md#s1-our-contributions-p2)).

    Eq. 11 is written over the dataset's pairs in the labelled order $a^+,a^-$. The paper does not say in words whether the loss uses the labels; its proof calls the pairs "the independent unlabeled responses" ([§C.3 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sC.3-p1)), and the labels enter it only through $\hat R$. The paper remarks that the reward model and the distillation "can come from different datasets" with the analysis "easily adapted"; step 25 has one such case.

    Supplementary (not from the inputs): exchanging the two responses turns both probabilities $p,q$ of a KL term into $1-p,1-q$, which leaves the term unchanged, so it does not depend on which response is labelled preferred.

    In the implementation, Algorithm 1 draws a fresh pair of responses for each prompt at each iteration from a "sampling distribution $\mu$", the symbol of Section 2, and the paper says this "can also be $\pi_t$", the current policy, and that "we also sample online responses from the distribution $\pi_t$" ([Algorithm 1](source/yun2025/paper.md#alg:pref_distill), [§D.2, Pseudocode ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sD.2-pseudocode-p1)). It does not say whether the dataset's pairs are also used. Table 1 lists preference distillation as requiring no RL training; as for PMLE in step 17, the paper does not say how this fits sampling from the policy being trained, the on-policy responses of step 2, and does not define "RL training". It files REBEL, whose regression it describes as repeated with on-policy responses, under "Without RL but with a reward model"; the REBEL work's title in the reference list is "Rebel: Reinforcement learning via regressing relative rewards".

24. <a id="step-24"></a>The guarantee needs one more assumption, since two classes are now involved: the policy class $\Pi$ and the class $\mathcal{P}_\gamma(\mathcal{R})$ of LMs induced by the reward models, and the assumption is that the second lies in the first ([§4, Convergence guarantee ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s4-convergence-guarantee-p1), [Assumption 5](source/yun2025/paper.md#assp:reward_model)). The induced LMs are non-generative (step 22); $\Pi$ is a class of distributions (step 12), and whether they can be sampled from does not enter the assumption. The paper gives two grounds ([§4, Convergence guarantee ¶2](source/yun2025/paper.md#s4-convergence-guarantee-p2)). The assumption "is related to" the generator-verifier gap, that verifying an answer is easier than generating one, which, it says, "implies" $|\mathcal{R}|\ll|\Pi|$ and "is speculated to hold for LLMs in practice"; the paper does not say how the gap gives the inclusion. And it "can also be justified by the fact that" reward models are often built on the frozen torso of the fine-tuned model; the paper does not say how this gives the inclusion either. A consequence used later is $C_{\mathcal{R}}\le C_\Pi$ for the coverage coefficient of the induced class: the constant of Definition 3 must hold for every model of a class, so it is no larger for a subclass.

    Theorem 6 gives the minimizer of Eq. 11 without the penalty the same bound as Theorem 4 ([Theorem 6](source/yun2025/paper.md#thm:pref_distill), [Eq. 13](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:distill_guarantee)). In the proof the first link of step 19 becomes two ([§C.3 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sC.3-p1)): the policy matches the simulator on every pair of the data, since by Assumption 5 the averaged KL can reach zero, and this bounds their distance on responses from $\mu$ ([Eq. 20](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:new_distill)); and the simulator, the PMLE estimate in the induced class, is bounded against $\pi^*$ by Proposition 12 ([Eq. 21](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:new_reward)). The rest follows step 19 ([Eq. 22](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:delta_bound_pref)). The proof of Proposition 12 uses that the minimizer's loss is at most that of $\pi^*$, which needs $\pi^*$ in the class over which the loss is minimized ([§C.2 ¶2](source/yun2025/paper.md#sC.2-p2)); here that class is the induced class, and the paper states no assumption that $\pi^*$ is in it (Assumption 1 puts $\pi^*$ in $\Pi$). The theorem's statement has $\ln(|\Pi|/\delta)$ alone, and the last display of the proof has also a term in $\ln|\mathcal{R}|$ ([§C.3 ¶2](source/yun2025/paper.md#sC.3-p2)); the paper does not say how that term goes.

25. <a id="step-25"></a>The bound being PMLE's, the guarantee shows no gain from distilling on the same data, and the paper says why: the policy learns from responses of $\mu$, "same as the reward model" ([§4, Benefits of distillation ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s4-benefits-of-distillation-p1)). The gain it claims is for a "stronger" base model $\pi_0$, from whose responses the policy can learn "instead", since the simulator can label any pair. For this case Theorem 14 gives the bound $\frac{1}{\gamma^2}\cdot\frac{C_0\ln(|\Pi|/\delta)+C_{\mathcal{R}}\ln(|\mathcal{R}|/\delta)}{n}$ ([Theorem 14](source/yun2025/paper.md#thm:banana), [Eq. 23](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:new_distill_guarantee)): the policy's distance from the simulator, now measured on responses from $\pi_0$ and moved to $\pi^*$ by a coefficient $C_0$, and the simulator's distance from $\pi^*$, moved by $C_{\mathcal{R}}$; $C_\Pi$ no longer appears ([§C.3 ¶3](source/yun2025/paper.md#sC.3-p3)). $C_0$ is not Definition 3 with $\pi_0$ for $\mu$ but a condition over pairs of models of $\Pi$ that a footnote calls "slightly stronger". The theorem's statement does not mention the reward model's data; Section 4 says the policy learns from responses of $\pi_0$ "instead" of those of $\mu$ on which the reward model is fitted; the proof uses one $n$ for both, and the paper does not say that the two sets of pairs have the same size.

    The claim is that this rate "improves over the PMLE rate if $\pi_0$ is comparatively well-aligned so that $C_0<C_\Pi$, thus rigorously establishing the benefits of distillation with a strong base model". As in step 10, the support is a comparison of upper bounds. $C_0<C_\Pi$ is assumed: the paper gives no argument that a well-aligned $\pi_0$ makes it hold, and the two coefficients are measured against different sources of responses. The paper does not say what makes a base model "stronger" or "well-aligned"; in this section it ties the words only to $C_0<C_\Pi$, and in Section 3 it posits that a well-aligned $\pi_0$ could improve the constants in $R$ ([step 20](#step-20)), without relating that to $C_0$. The condition concerns the first term; for the second the claim states none, and Section 5 says that under Assumption 5 it "may" be much smaller than $C_\Pi\ln|\Pi|$, writing "the improved preference distillation guarantee" as $C_0\ln|\Pi|$ alone ([§5, Why does reverse KL attain a better bound? ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s5-why-does-reverse-kl-attain-a-better-bound-p1)).

    The proof is written out up to a point and ends "The remainder of the proof proceeds similarly" ([§C.3 ¶3](source/yun2025/paper.md#sC.3-p3)). It uses Proposition 12 on the induced class with the same unstated condition as step 24, and it passes from pairs to single responses by an identity that needs the quantity to have mean zero under the responses' distribution; the rewards are centered under $\mu$ (step 15) and the responses here come from $\pi_0$, and the paper does not say why the identity holds. No experiment is presented as a test of Theorem 14; the implementation samples the policy's responses from the policy itself ([step 23](#step-23)).

## 5 Reverse KL Minimization Approach

26. <a id="step-26"></a>Section 5 changes the divergence minimized. The two methods so far maximize a preference likelihood and have forward-KL guarantees; the paper calls it "also plausible" to minimize the reverse KL ([§5 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s5-p1)), for its "well-known" mode-seeking behaviour, as against the forward KL's mode covering; mode seeking, it says, "tends to find distributions that generate realistic content and has been preferred in image generative models", citing two works on image generation. This is cited; the paper does not show mode seeking for language models, and its guarantee for the method is again on the forward KL ([step 31](#step-31)).

    Supplementary (not from the inputs): the reverse KL averages over the learned model's own samples, so it penalizes putting probability where the target has little and tends to concentrate on the target's high-probability responses ("mode seeking"); the forward KL averages over the target's samples and penalizes missing any of them ("mode covering").

27. <a id="step-27"></a>The objective follows in three moves ([§5 ¶2](source/yun2025/paper.md#s5-p2)). The reverse KL is the policy's expected $-\ln\pi^*$ minus its entropy ([Eq. 14](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:rkl_ideal)), which needs $-\ln\pi^*$, "the very object we are trying to estimate". The paper puts in the estimate $\tilde\pi$ from "a surrogate class of language models that are easier to train but harder to sample from", the PMLE minimizer over the induced class "(with a suitable regularization)", which it does not specify, equivalently the reward model of step 22; the normalizer does not depend on the policy and drops out, "as we only require relative rewards". It adds the KL regularizer toward $\pi_0$ "again", with no reason beyond that word, which refers to PMLE and preference distillation (steps 17 and 23). The result is RLHF's objective plus $\gamma$ times the entropy, averaged over the dataset's prompts only ([Eq. 15](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:rkl)). In the equation $\gamma$ weights the entropy and $\beta$ the KL term; the sentence after it says "$\beta$ and $\gamma$ control the relative weights of the policy entropy and KL regularizer, respectively", in the opposite order. In practice, the paper says, one fits $\hat R$ "to approximate the underlying true reward $R^*$" and then minimizes Eq. 15 by RL such as PPO; Section 2 defines $R_*=\gamma\ln\pi^*$ ([§2, Theoretical setup ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s2-theoretical-setup-p1)), and the paper does not say whether this $R^*$ is that reward. Table 1 lists RKL as requiring RL.

28. <a id="step-28"></a>Since Eq. 15 is RLHF's objective with one more term, the paper draws a claim about RLHF ([§5, Relation to RLHF ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s5-relation-to-rlhf-p1)). "At face value" RKL generalizes RLHF, which is RKL at $\gamma=0$; conversely, "under our preference assumption", RLHF "can be interpreted as minimizing a reverse KL in the limit $\gamma\to0$", a statement given in one sentence with no derivation. Hence the RKL result "can be viewed as providing theoretical justification" for the RLHF objective, "while also providing a minor correction"; the abstract says "providing strong justification for RLHF" ([Abstract ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#abstract-p1)). The paper says the connection "may not be surprising", since maximum-entropy RL "can be seen as reverse KL minimization" ([reference ziebart10modeling](source/yun2025/paper.md#ref-ziebart10modeling)); that link is cited and not new, and what the section adds is the reading of $\hat R$ as an estimate of $\gamma\ln\pi^*$ (step 22) and the guarantee of [step 30](#step-30). That guarantee is for $\gamma>0$ and $\beta=0$, its second term carries $1/\gamma^2$, and the paper does not say what it gives as $\gamma\to0$, the limit in which it reads RLHF as reverse KL minimization. On RLHF itself the paper offers the toy experiment of step 9 and the argument of the next step.

29. <a id="step-29"></a>The paper restates the dilemma of step 3 as an asymptotic dichotomy of RLHF, "assuming $\mu$ has sufficient coverage" ([§5, The dilemma of RLHF ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s5-the-dilemma-of-rlhf-p1)): a fixed $\beta>0$ underfits, keeping the model near $\pi_0$, and $\beta_n\downarrow0$ collapses it to a degenerate solution; "Neither is desirable." This is argued, with no formal statement, and "sufficient coverage" is not defined. RKL's answer is Theorem 7, for $\beta=0$, where its objective targets $\tilde\pi$, a distribution over all responses (step 7). The paper says the dichotomy "can also be seen" in Figure 1, and that "as demonstrated in Theorem 7" the forward KL of its approach tends to zero while RLHF's "eventually saturates at a non-zero value". The theorem is for RKL at $\beta=0$ and the figure for the best $\beta$ at each $n$, which is not reported (step 9); the figure is the only evidence for the RLHF side.

30. <a id="step-30"></a>Theorem 7 is for the objective without the regularizer: its minimizer has, with probability at least $1-\delta$, forward KL error $\lesssim\frac{\ln(|\Pi|/\delta)}{n}+\frac{C_{\mathcal{R}}}{\gamma^2}\cdot\frac{\ln(|\mathcal{R}|/\delta)}{n}$ ([§5, Convergence guarantee ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s5-convergence-guarantee-p1), [Theorem 7](source/yun2025/paper.md#thm:reverse_kl), [Eq. 16](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:rev_guarantee)). The two terms are those of step 24, the policy against the simulator and the simulator against the target; the first now carries no coverage coefficient and no $1/\gamma^2$. The reason is in the proof ([§C.4 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sC.4-p1)): at $\beta=0$ the objective is, up to constants, the KL from the policy to $\tilde\pi$ over the dataset's prompts, which reaches zero by Assumption 5, so the policy equals $\tilde\pi$ at every response on those prompts; this gives what the paper calls "a stronger sup norm bound which is guaranteed due to realizability" ([Eq. 24](source/yun2025/paper.md#eq:summer)), which holds on responses from $\pi^*$ with no change of distribution. The second term repeats Theorem 4 on the induced class, with the unstated condition of step 24 that $\pi^*$ is in it. The hidden factors depend on $R$, and in the second term also on $\gamma$; the paper gives no value of $C_{\mathcal{R}}$, $|\Pi|$, $|\mathcal{R}|$ or $R$ for language models.

31. <a id="step-31"></a>The paper explains why this bound is better and why it bounds the forward KL ([§5, Why does reverse KL attain a better bound? ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s5-why-does-reverse-kl-attain-a-better-bound-p1), [¶2](source/yun2025/paper.md#s5-why-does-reverse-kl-attain-a-better-bound-p2)).

    - **The comparison.** The bound has the coverage coefficient of the induced class, not of $\Pi$, and under Assumption 5, the paper says, $\ln|\Pi|$ and $C_{\mathcal{R}}\ln|\mathcal{R}|$ "may both be much smaller than" $C_\Pi\ln|\Pi|$, "or even" $C_0\ln|\Pi|$ (Theorem 14). The grounds of step 24, $C_{\mathcal{R}}\le C_\Pi$ and $|\mathcal{R}|\ll|\Pi|$, concern the second term; for the first the paper gives no reason. The sentence writes the terms without the factor $1/\gamma^2$, which the first terms of Theorems 4, 6 and 14 carry and that of Theorem 7 does not (step 30). "May" is the paper's hedge, and what is compared is two upper bounds; this is the comparison behind [step 10](#step-10).
    - **Why RKL avoids $C_\Pi$.** Preference distillation's policy step still uses response pairs from $\mu$ (or $\pi_0$), whereas RKL uses responses only in "the lightweight reward modeling step"; in the proofs, agreement on the data's pairs against agreement at every response on its prompts (steps 24 and 30). The responses meant are the data. The paper does not relate this to the RL algorithm of step 27, which samples from the policy; in Theorem 7 the expectation over the policy's responses is part of the objective and $\hat\pi$ its exact minimizer.
    - **Why the forward KL is bounded.** Doing so "even though the objective is derived from minimizing reverse KL" avoids comparing the coverage of $\hat\pi$ against $\mu$, "which is not guaranteed by Definition 3"; the paper does not say why a reverse-KL bound would need that comparison. The two directions can be compared at a factor exponential in $R$ ([Proposition 15](source/yun2025/paper.md#prop:klsame)).

    The paper does not minimize the forward KL from $\tilde\pi$ directly, though that would use no response pairs and so not pay $C_\Pi$, because it is intractable: it needs a sum over all of $\mathcal{A}$, samples from an unnormalized model, or the values of $\tilde\pi$ ([§5, Why does reverse KL attain a better bound? ¶3](source/yun2025/paper.md#s5-why-does-reverse-kl-attain-a-better-bound-p3), [§B, Intractability of forward KL ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sB-intractability-of-forward-kl-p1)). This is argued; no bound is proved for it.

## 6 Experiments

32. <a id="step-32"></a>Section 6 asks whether the derived objectives also work as training methods: its "evaluation point" is whether objectives "derived purely from our distribution-learning assumption" "remain competitive with strong empirical baselines (DPO, REBEL, RLHF) in practice?" ([§6 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s6-p1), [§6 ¶2](source/yun2025/paper.md#s6-p2)). The paper does not say what result would count as competitive. Each method is compared with its baseline of step 7. What is measured changes: the theorems bound the forward KL to $\pi^*$, which the toy of step 9 measures with $\pi^*$ known; here $\pi^*$ is not available, and what is measured is response quality judged by a language model, and in summarization also a reward-model score and the divergence from $\pi_0$. The experiments test neither Eq. 2 nor the bounds, and the paper does not present them as a test of either.

Conditions of the summarization experiment ([§6.1 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s6.1-p1), [§D.2, Dataset ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sD.2-dataset-p1), [§D.2, Hyperparameters ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sD.2-hyperparameters-p1), [Table 5](source/yun2025/paper.md#tab:tldr_hparams)): TL;DR, with Pythia-2.8B and Pythia-6.9B; win-rate against human reference summaries judged by GPT-4 over 600 test samples; a reward-model score from "the trained reward model", which the paper does not identify or say is the one the four methods with a reward model are trained against; three seeds. DPO and PMLE train on the preference dataset for one epoch, the others on online responses for about 8.5 epochs. Sampling is at temperature 0.1 for DPO and PMLE and 0.7 for the others, and the paper does not say whether for evaluation, training or both. Table 5 gives RKL an "entropy coefficient" and no parameter named $\gamma$, and the paper does not say this coefficient is the $\gamma$ of Eq. 15.

33. <a id="step-33"></a>On the win-rate, which the paper calls "the most direct measure of LM quality", each method is above its baseline for both models ("higher win-rates than their respective baselines in this experiment"), by 1.0 to 3.4 points, against standard deviations over three seeds of 0.5 to 1.5 points; no significance test is reported ([§6.1, Results ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s6.1-results-p1), [Table 2](source/yun2025/paper.md#tab:tldr)). The other two columns, better when higher (reward-model score) and when lower (divergence), do not all favour the paper's methods: PMLE's score is below DPO's for Pythia-2.8B and RKL's below RLHF's for Pythia-6.9B, and for Pythia-2.8B preference distillation and RKL have higher divergences than REBEL and RLHF. The paper concludes that the experiment gives "evidence that the algorithms derived from our assumption [...] can remain competitive with popular baselines". It compares each method only with its own baseline; across pairs the table has the reward-model methods well above DPO and PMLE, but the paper does not use this as evidence for the finding of step 10, and the two groups also differ in sampling temperature and training length.

34. <a id="step-34"></a>The divergence column bears on the remark of step 18. PMLE's divergence from $\pi_0$ is less than half DPO's for both models, which the paper attributes to PMLE's regularizer computed "with online data", where DPO "solely relies on the offline dataset", and says agrees with Song et al.'s results without saying which ([§6.1, Results ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s6.1-results-p1), [Table 2](source/yun2025/paper.md#tab:tldr)). These are the conditions behind step 18: PMLE at $\beta=10^{-5}$ with the penalty on online responses (step 17), DPO at Table 5's "$\beta$" of 0.05. The paper does not say how the divergence was estimated. It reports no run of PMLE without the regularizer here, so its attribution is not tested against one; the two objectives also differ inside the sigmoid (step 18). For RLHF and REBEL the paper says the same KL penalty "naturally" gives similar divergences; Table 2 has 24.94 and 25.67 for Pythia-2.8B and 24.38 and 29.80 for Pythia-6.9B. Table 5 gives both a "kl coefficient", though the REBEL loss of Eq. 10 has no KL term, and the paper does not say how the penalty enters it. The divergences of preference distillation and RKL against their baselines are not discussed.

Conditions of the general chat experiment ([§6.2 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s6.2-p1), [§D.3, Dataset and Models ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sD.3-dataset-and-models-p1), [§D.3, Hyperparameters ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sD.3-hyperparameters-p1), [Table 6](source/yun2025/paper.md#tab:chat_hparams)): LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct trained on UltraFeedback with the existing reward model ArmoRM-Llama3-8B-v0.1; only PMLE against DPO and preference distillation against REBEL, "since these differ algorithmically from their baselines, whereas RKL only adds an entropy term to the same PPO update as RLHF"; PMLE with its KL coefficient at 0.0, and preference distillation with the $\gamma$ of the summarization experiment, not tuned again; quality judged by an unnamed LLM on MT-Bench, AlpacaEval 2.0 and Arena Hard; no run count or spread reported.

35. <a id="step-35"></a>In general chat both methods are above their baselines on AlpacaEval 2.0 and Arena Hard, and on MT-Bench PMLE is 0.02 below DPO and preference distillation 0.01 above REBEL ([§6.2, Results ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s6.2-results-p1), [Table 3](source/yun2025/paper.md#tab:academic)). The gains on AlpacaEval 2.0 are 9.51 (length-controlled) and 6.49 (raw) points for PMLE and 2.45 and 1.87 for preference distillation; on Arena Hard 1.4 and 2.1. The paper calls the results "encouraging": the two methods "perform favorably" against DPO and REBEL "across the evaluated benchmarks", with "particularly strong alignment-quality gains on AlpacaEval 2.0 and Arena Hard while remaining comparable on MT-Bench", and says the two experiments together "suggest" the objectives are "not only competitive with strong baselines, but also promising"; it adds that preference distillation's results "might be conservative" since its $\gamma$ was not tuned. The paper does not say how or on what data the reward model was trained, or relate it to the reading of step 22. PMLE runs without its regularizer, the one difference from DPO the paper names (step 18); the other difference, inside the sigmoid, remains, and the paper does not say what algorithmic difference it means. The appendix's opening promises "additional results on MT-Bench and AlpacaEval 2.0" ([§D ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#sD-p1)); Appendix D.3 has none beyond Table 3.

36. <a id="step-36"></a>The paper words its empirical claim in three ways: the introduction, that the methods "generally outperform baseline win-rates in TL;DR summarization and generate more preferred responses in general chat" ([§1, Our contributions ¶4](source/yun2025/paper.md#s1-our-contributions-p4)); the abstract, that "empirical results indicate" they are "competitive with strong baselines across several tasks and models" ([Abstract ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#abstract-p1)); the conclusion, "competitive performance and gains in several TL;DR and chat-evaluation settings" ([§7 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s7-p1)). The evidence covers two tasks, three models, all judgments made by language models; RKL is run in summarization only. Against Table 2, "outperform baseline win-rates in TL;DR" holds for the means of all six pairs, with no significance test. Against Table 3, "more preferred responses in general chat" holds for both methods on AlpacaEval 2.0 and Arena Hard, and on MT-Bench PMLE is 0.02 below DPO. The paper does not reconcile its wordings or say whether "outperform" or "competitive" is its claim, and it does not define "competitive" (step 32).

## 7 Conclusion

37. <a id="step-37"></a>The conclusion restates the paper and names what it leaves open ([§7 ¶1](source/yun2025/paper.md#s7-p1)). It describes alignment formulated as distribution learning "from a single explicit assumption", Eq. 3, and three methods that "correct and generalize existing approaches" with $O(1/n)$ forward-KL guarantees, "accompanied by empirical results showing competitive performance and gains in several TL;DR and chat-evaluation settings" (steps 8 and 36). Of its own methods the paper uses "correction" and "generalization" only for RKL against RLHF ([step 28](#step-28)); it does not say what PMLE or preference distillation corrects or generalizes, beside the differences in steps 18 and 23. The guarantees also rest on Assumptions 1, 2 and 5 and on the coverage coefficients, which the sentence does not repeat. Three future directions follow. Comparing mode-seeking and mode-covering objectives "across domains": the paper does not say which domains or objectives it means; it discusses the two behaviours only in its reason for the reverse KL, which rests on cited work on images ([step 26](#step-26)), ties mode covering only to the forward KL, which it does not minimize directly ([step 31](#step-31)), and does not compare the two for language models. Extending the framework to "alternative divergences and preference models": for preference models this is the extension step 14 says the paper claims and does not give; for divergences it made no earlier claim, and Section 2 leaves the measure open (step 12). "Tightening the exponential dependence on $R$ via stronger assumptions on $\pi_0$" is the conjecture of step 20, and the factor is the one hidden in the bounds (step 19).
